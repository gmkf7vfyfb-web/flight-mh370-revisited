1.0 INTRODUCTION  
On July 6, 2013 at 11:28 am Pacific daylight time, a Boeing 777-200ER, registration HL7742, 
operated by Asiana Airlines as flight 214, struck the seawall short of runway 28L at San 
Francisco International Airport. The airplane was destroyed by impact forces and fire. Three 
of the 291 passengers were fatally injured. The flight was a regularly scheduled passenger 
flight from Incheon International Airport (RKSI), Seoul, Korea, and was operated under the 
provisions of 14 Code of Federal Regulations  Part 129. Visual meteorological conditions 
prevailed at the time of the accident. 
The event flight time alignment between the Co ckpit Voice Recorder (CVR), Flight Data 
Recorder (FDR), Air Traffic Control (ATC) communication recordings, ATC Airport 
Surveillance Radar (ASR), and ATC Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast (ADS-B) 
data was determined. The flight 214 weight and balance records and component weight 
buildup was examined. A comparison of the maximum landing gear and flaps extended

speed placards to the documented flight 214 landing gear and flap configurations indicates 
no landing gear or flap extension placard exceedances. The B777-200ER low speed amber 
band parameter time history was reconstructed and compared to available FDR data for 
minimum maneuvering speed during the flight 214 approach and CVR data for an EICAS 
caution alert event and a stick shaker activation event. Finally, calculated pilot eye height, 
calculated Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) guidance, and select FDR parameters 
were plotted to visualize the airplane flight path and energy state as a function of Asiana 
flight 214 distance from the runway 28L displaced threshold and time prior to impact. 
 
The NTSB and the Boeing calculated minimum maneuver speed profiles compare favorably 
(within about 1 to 4 knots) with the sparse Flight Management Computer (FMC) minimum 
maneuver speed data recorded on the FDR. In addition, the calculated timing results for the 
expected Engine Indicating and Crew Alerting System (EICAS) caution alert AIRSPEED 
LOW and the stick shaker activation events appear to match the available CVR data within 
expected timing tolerances for each event.  
 
 
2.0 TECHNICAL METHODS 
 
The data sources and technical methods used in this study are described in this section. 
 
 
2.1 Time Alignment 
 
The CVR, FDR, ASR radar, ADS-B, ATC communications recordings, ACARS, and video 
surveillance camera data are each recorded as a function of time. However, the “timestamp” 
value associated with these data is not necessarily  explicit. Moreover, data from two or more 
independent sources that record explicit timestamp information are generally not perfectly 
synchronized to a common or desired reference time basis. In particular, the CVR and the 
FDR do not explicitly record a time value with each respective event or data sample. In 
contrast, each record in a radar data log, ATC transcript, many security video frames, and 
ACARS entry is associated with an explicit time value, but the time reference used for each 
may not be synchronized to a common reference time basis. 
 
The time alignment process yields one or more equations that correlate recorded information 
from independent sources to a reference clock, thereby defining the accident event sequence 
and elapsed time between events. The reference clock selected is generally the ATC radar or 
ADS-B data time. In this study the reference clock was based on the ADS-B data time. 
 
The ability to correlate information between any two independent sources is dependent on 
the existence of at least one common event between the two sources. In practice, the FDR 
discrete parameters that indicate the activation of the Captain’s or First Officer’s VHF radio 
transmit keys provide common event sources because the begin- and end-keying events are 
typically recorded on the CVR and the FDR. The communication that occurs between the 
begin- and end-keying event is typically recorded on both the CVR and the ATC recordings. 
 
 
2.1.1 Aircraft Recorder Data 
 
The CVR transcript data and the FDR data for the accident flight are documented in the 
Cockpit Voice Recorder Factual Report and Transcript and the Flight Data Recorder Group 
Chairman’s Factual Report, respectively.

2.1.2 CVR and FDR Alignment 
 
Common events on the CVR and FDR were identified and used to define the CVR/FDR time 
alignment constraints. Each FDR common event has an associated begin and end time value 
due to the discrete nature of the FDR data. Corresponding CVR common events are 
associated with a CVR time value which must be precisely measured. The points on a 
common event plot of FDR time versus CVR time define the constraints. A curve that 
satisfies all the constraints defines a mapping from CVR time to FDR time. 
 
 
2.1.3 FDR and ATC Alignment 
 
The existence of one or more common events between the ATC transcript and the CVR and 
the CVR/FDR time alignment permits the FDR data to be aligned to the ATC transcript. Time 
alignment between the ATC transcript and the FDR data is accomplished by choosing a 
specific ATC clock time to equal a specific FDR time. 
 
 
2.1.4 FDR and Radar/ADS-B Data Alignment 
 
The FDR and ADS-B data were time aligned using the FDR pressure altitude and the ADS-B 
altitude information. Given local barometric pressure and knowledge of the pressure 
reference used on the aircraft, the FDR pressure altitude was corrected for atmospheric 
deviation from standard day conditions. The ADS-B and FDR time alignment was then 
accomplished by 1) creating an overlay plot of the pressure altitude versus time from each 
source, 2) adding a +/-50 foot error band to the ADS-B altitude data, and 3) time shifting the 
ADS-B data with respect to the corrected FDR altitude data to a point where the FDR altitude 
curve was bounded by the +/-50 foot error band throughout the time period of interest. 
 
 
2.1.5 FDR and Security Video Alignment 
 
Security camera video recorded at the KFSO Security Operations Center and other video 
sources documented in the Attachments 11-21 of the Aircraft Performance Group Crash Site 
Factual Report have not been explicitly time aligned to date. 
 
 
2.2 Airplane Weight and Balance 
 
The accident aircraft weight was estimated by itemizing the aircraft component weights. 
Component weights include the aircraft empty weight, the crew weights including nominal 
crew baggage, passenger weights including carry-on baggage and personal effects, cargo, 
and fuel. The component weight buildup approach sums the component weights to arrive at a 
weight estimate for the event condition, accounting for weight changes from a reference 
condition. The HL7742 reference condition is the aircraft weight just prior to flight 214 engine 
start. Given the component weights and locations, the aircraft moment balance can be 
calculated. 
 
The longitudinal center of gravity position in  percent Mean Aerodynamic Chord (%MAC) and 
Index Units (I.U.) can be calculated using the following equations, given the moment arm in 
inches and the weight (for the latter case): 
 
%MAC       = { [ARM (Inch) - 1174.5] / 278.5 } x 100 
 
Index Units = { WT (lb) x [ARM (Inch) - 1258] } / 400,000 + 50

2.2.1 Operating Empty Weight 
 
According to Asiana Airlines weight and balance record AAR-QA-529-02, the HL7742 basic 
empty weight on September 6, 2007 was 309,752.47 pounds with the center of gravity 
located at 1232.42 inches aft of the referenc e datum. Asiana Airlines computes individual 
aircraft weights in accordance with their weight and balance control program. The flight 214 
load sheet reflects a specific aircraft operating empty weight of 325,008 pounds, which 
includes crew weights for 2 flight crewmem bers, 12 flight attendants, and standard operating 
items. 
 
 
2.2.2 Crew and Crew Baggage Weights 
 
Asiana Airlines uses crew weights (including bags) of 210 pounds per flight crewmember and 
165 pounds per flight attendant, consistent with the guidance published in their weight and 
balance control program. 
 
 
2.2.3 Passenger Weights 
 
The Asiana Airlines average weight method for international flights accounts for nominal 
weights of 75 kg (165.3 pounds) per adult, 37 kg (81.6 pounds) per child (between 2 years 
and less than 13 years of age), and 10 kg (22 pounds) per infant (less than 24 months old). 
The average weight method includes a 22 pound-per-passenger allowance for carry-on 
baggage and personal effects.  
 
The flight 214 load sheet reflects a total of 291 passengers (260 adults, 30 children, and 1 
infant) and 2 reserve flight crewmembers on board. The total weight of 45,790 pounds for 
293 persons is based on 262 adults (262 x 75 = 19,650 kg), 30 children (30 x 37 = 1,110 kg), 
and 1 infant (1 x 10 = 10 kg) which yields 20,770 kg (45,790 pounds). 
 
 
2.2.4 Fuel Weight 
 
According to the flight 214 load sheet, the planned takeoff fuel for flight 214 was 193,200 
pounds. Asiana Airlines assumes a 1,000 pound nominal taxi fuel burn for the Boeing 777, 
yielding a ramp weight of 194,200 pounds. 
 
Prior to flight 214, HL7742 completed flight OZ111 from Kansai International Airport (RJBB) 
to Incheon and arrived with 14,100 pounds of fuel remaining based on ACARS data. A fuel 
receipt record documented in the Aircraft Performance Group Accident Site Factual Report 
shows that a total of 27,685 gallons of fuel (density 6.58 pounds per gallon) were uploaded in 
Incheon prior to the flight 214 departure. Based on the reported fuel density, HL7742 would 
have had 196,267 pounds of fuel (remaining fuel plus fuel added is 14,100 + 27685 x 6.58) 
available at flight 214 engine start for nominal conditions. The FDR data at engine start 
indicate 62,200, 62,500, and 69,700 pounds of fuel in the left wing tank, right wing tank, and 
center tank, respectively, for a total ramp fuel load of 194,400 pounds.  
 
During the post-accident airplane recovery effort, the fuelers reported that the center fuel 
tank was empty, the number 1 fuel tank indicated between 5,000 and 6,000 pounds, and the 
number 2 fuel tank showed about 8,000 pounds. The fuelers noted that the airplane was not 
in a wings level attitude when these fuel values were measured.

2.2.5 Cargo Weight 
 
Asiana Airlines accounts for actual weight s for cargo and checked baggage. For flight 214 
the load sheet documents 304 checked bags that weighed 10,245 pounds (33.7 pounds per 
bag average) and 12,461 pounds of freight. The total cargo compartment load of 22,706

compartment 4; and 111 pounds in compartment 5.  
 
 
2.2.6 Boeing Estimate of Airplane Weight and Center of Gravity Position during the Flight 
 
At NTSB request Boeing Engineering estimated the flight 214 weight and balance based on 
the following fuel burn assumptions during the flight: 
 
a) Burn fuel from the center tanks until the center tanks are empty. In practice, some fuel 
remains in the center tanks at the time that fuel is switched to burn from the main 
tanks. However, the remaining center tank fuel is scavenged to the main tanks while 
burning from the mains. Fuel remaining in the center tanks when the tanks are 
switched has no effect on the time of arrival center of gravity calculation because that 
fuel would have been completely scavenged from the center tanks by the time the 
airplane lands.  
 
b) Burn fuel from the main tanks for the remainder of the flight. 
 
c) Center of gravity calculations equated to a flaps up configuration with landing gear 
retracted. Extending flaps and landing gear has a negligible net effect because the 
combined flap and landing gear center of gravity change is small. It is on the order of 
0.5 %MAC in the aft direction.  
 
d)  Fuel density is 6.8 pounds/gallon. 
 
 
2.3 Speed Tape Parameter Calculations (Low Speed Amber Band) 
 
Boeing provided the NTSB with the technical specifications required to reconstruct the speed 
tape time history of low speed amber band information expected to be presented to the flight 
crewmembers during the approach. The top of amber band speed is set to the Maneuver 
Margin above Stick Shaker (V SSNG) value, which can be equated to a normal load factor 
maneuvering capability of 1.3 g. The minimum speed limit (V MIN) is set to the computed Stick 
Shaker Speed (V SS) value, which corresponds to the junction between the bottom of the 
amber band and the top of the red dashed line where stick shaker is scheduled to activate. 
Boeing and NTSB engineers independently implem ented the relevant speed tape parameter 
technical specifications1 using the available Asiana flight 214 FDR data. 
 
For the 777-200ER, the calculated stick shaker speed is a function of the following variables: 
angle of attack, Mach number, calibrated airspeed, slat position, flap position, landing gear 
position, anti-ice configuration, speedbrake position, and engine thrust. Calculated stick 
shaker speed values are filtered prior to presentation to the flight crewmembers on the speed 
tape. For calculation purposes, if the slats are in-between detents, they are assumed to be in 
the lesser (less extended) detent. In contrast, if the flaps are in-between detents, they are 
assumed to be in the higher (more extended) detent.

Stick shaker is activated if the angle of attack equals or exceeds the calculated stick shaker 
trip point angle of attack (AOA SS_TRIP) and the airplane Air Mode Logic yields a value of 
Airplane In Air. The computed value of unfiltered stick shaker speed (VSS_UNFILTERED) is latched 
if the angle of attack equals or exceeds AOA SS_TRIP and the last calculated value of 
VSS_UNFILTERED equals or exceeds the current calibrated airspeed. The V SS_UNFILTERED value will 
remain latched until either the calibrated airspeed exceeds V SS_UNFILTERED or the angle of 
attack decreases below AOA SS_TRIP. If either of these unlatching conditions occurs, a new 
value of VSS_UNFILTERED is calculated. 
 
The top of amber band speed is set to the calculated Maneuver Margin above Stick Shaker 
value and is a function of V SS_UNFILTERED, body-axis longitudinal acceleration, and body-axis 
normal acceleration. Calculated V SSNG values are filtered prior to presentation to the flight 
crewmembers on the speed tape.

2.5 Pilot Eye Height and Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) Guidance 
 
The pilot eye height parameter calculated by Boeing Engineering is based on airplane 
geometric altitude at the center of gravity with pitch and roll attitude adjustments to account 
for the pilot eye station. For calculation purposes, the pilot eye station is located 88.5 feet 
forward of the center of gravity and 3.33 feet above the center of gravity.  
 
The Aircraft Performance Group Crash Site Factual Report documents the necessary data 
and calculations to determine the PAPI beam pos ition relative to the runway 28L displaced 
threshold. For this study, the PAPI beam angle (2.98°) and intermediate deviation angles 
(average values of ±0.185°) were based on the applicable July 2, 2013 FAA flight inspection 
report results. The PAPI maximum deviation angles (±0.583°) were based on the FAA 
guidance for Height Group 4 aircraft (Table 7-1 from AC 150/5340-30G). The longitudinal 
distance from the PAPI installation to the runway 28L displaced threshold is 1,366.5 feet.

The Boeing and NTSB amber band parameter calculation results 3 shown in Figures 2–3 are 
nearly identical. Both calculated minimum maneuver speed profiles compare favorably (within 
about 1 to 4 knots) with the sparse Flight Management Computer (FMC) minimum maneuver 
speed data recorded on the FDR. Differences between calculated (NTSB and Boeing) and 
recorded (flight 214 FDR) minimum maneuver speed parameters are likely due in part to the 
coarse resolution4 of the FDR angle of attack data (about ±0.352°).  
 
The sensitivity of the speed tape parameter calculations to the FDR combined left and right 
angle of attack resolution was estimated by adding a constant value of one-half the FDR 
angle of attack resolution (0.5 times 0.352) to the FDR-based angle of attack input signal at 
each time step and repeating the minimum maneuver speed calculation. The speed 
sensitivity to angle of attack results are plotted in Figures 4 and 5 with the added purple line 
identified as V SSNG_dAOA=+0.175_deg_FILTERED_(NTSB_CALC.). The addition of one-half the angle of 
attack parameter resolution yields a calculated minimum maneuver speed increase of about 
1 to 4 knots for the available FDR comparison points. 
 
Although the results are not shown in Figures 2 or 4, Boeing Engineering also reconstructed 
the V SS_FILTERED and V SSNG_FILTERED values for the earlier flaps-up portion of the descent 
(including the speed-brakes-up period). The Boeing and NTSB results reportedly differ by 
less than 1 knot, with the largest difference found during the speedbrake retraction transient. 
  
In a separate study, Boeing Engineering used Warning Electronics System (WES) hardware, 
which calculates the V SS_FILTERED and V SSNG_FILTERED values onboard the airplane, in a 
laboratory bench test to validate the analytical calculation method outlined in Section 2.3. 
The Boeing analytical and hardware WES results matched well, with only small differences 
observed during dynamic conditions. Boeing E ngineering attributed these small differences 
to filter implementation details and considered them to be insignificant for analytical method 
validation purposes. 
 
In response to an NTSB request, Boeing Engineering provided a list of EICAS caution alerts 
that could result in a quadruple chime aural event on the accident airplane. The FDR data 
and the Boeing and NTSB speed tape parameter reconstruction results are consistent with 
an EICAS caution alert AIRSPEED LOW at FDR Subframe Reference Number (SRN) 
97899.6. The CVR data indicate an aural quadruple chime event at FDR SRN 97900.7. A 
Boeing study found that the time delay between the calculated EICAS caution alert 
AIRSPEED LOW and the resulting aural quadruple chime on the aircraft should not exceed 
1.8 seconds. 
 
The FDR data and the Boeing and NTSB speed tape parameter reconstruction results are 
also consistent with stick shaker activation at FDR SRN 97907.6. The CVR data indicate that 
an aural stick shaker event began at FDR SRN 97907.8 and continued for at least 2.2 
seconds. The calculated times for the Boeing and NTSB EICAS caution alert AIRSPEED 
LOW and expected stick shaker activation in this study are based on zero parameter

4.0 SUMMARY 
 
The event flight time alignment between the CVR, FDR, ATC communication recordings, 
ADS-B, and ASR radar data was determined. Based on the load sheet component weight 
and balance buildup and the FDR weight parameters, the center of gravity at time of arrival 
was located at about 28.5 %MAC, near the middle of the envelope. A comparison of the 
maximum landing gear and flaps extended speed placards to the documented flight 214 
landing gear and flap configurations indicates no landing gear or flap extension placard 
exceedances. 
 
The NTSB and the Boeing calculated minimum maneuver speed profiles compare favorably 
(within about 1 to 4 knots) with the sparse Flight Management Computer (FMC) minimum 
maneuver speed data recorded on the FDR. In addition, the calculated timing results for the 
expected EICAS caution alert AIRSPEED LOW and the stick shaker activation events appear 
to match the available CVR data within expected timing tolerances for each event. Calculated 
pilot eye height, calculated PAPI guidance, a nd select FDR parameters were plotted to 
visualize the airplane flight path and energy state as a function of Asiana flight 214 distance 
from the runway 28L displaced threshold and time prior to impact.
