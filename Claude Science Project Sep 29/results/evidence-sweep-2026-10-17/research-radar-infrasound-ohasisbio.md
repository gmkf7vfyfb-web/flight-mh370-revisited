Prepared 17 October 2026. All times are UTC unless stated. Local time in Aceh (WIB) is UTC+7; in Malaysia (MYT) UTC+8.

## Q1. Indonesian, Indian and Thai military radar, 18:00–19:30 UTC

**Short answer.** No official document I found says whether the Indonesian Air Force radars at Lhokseumawe (Radar Unit 231) or Sabang were switched on between 18:00 and 19:30 UTC. Indonesian officials said in March–April 2014 that both radars were checked and did not detect MH370. The Air Force spokesman also said TNI-AU military radars operate 24 hours a day [press]. The SIR records only two Indonesian facts. First, the Medan ATC radar (range 240 NM) "for unknown reasons" did not pick up SSR code A2157. Second, the Indonesian military stated that they picked up MH370 earlier, as it headed towards IGARI (SIR printed p. 18) [official]. The Medan non-detection is not informative, because the transponder had stopped by 17:21. The claim that the Indonesian radars were off at night comes only from independent analysts and journalists (Iannello 2015; Wise 2019; Godfrey 2021, 2024), and none of them gives a primary source. Godfrey's statement that "Indonesia issued a report" on a maintenance shutdown could not be traced: **NOT FOUND**. I found no document saying that any Indonesian radar recorded other traffic on N571 near MEKAR at 18:00–18:30: **NOT FOUND**. The SIR main volume and the ATSB 2014/2015 search-area report do not mention EK343, IndiGo or any other aircraft near MEKAR. The EK343 position (about 38 NM behind, 493 kt, FL340 at 18:22) appears only in a 2017 blog comment based on FlightAware/Flightradar24, and another commenter disputed the timing [independent analyst]. For India, the Andaman and Nicobar Command chief of staff said the radars may have been off because they operate "as required" [press]. For Thailand, the Royal Thai Air Force reported a sporadic unknown target at 17:28 UTC. It was later seen "swinging north and disappearing over the Andaman Sea", with no times given [press].

**Implication for the estimate.** An Indonesian non-detection can enter the likelihood only if P(radar operating and recording at 18:00–19:30) is known. No public evidence fixes that probability. A record of EK343 or other N571 traffic in the Indonesian data would show that the radar was operating, but no such record is public.

## Q1b. Indian radars that could have been in range after 18:22 UTC

**Short answer.** No official Indian source lists the air-surveillance radars on the Andaman and Nicobar Islands in 2014, their types or their instrumented ranges: **NOT FOUND**. Only the military airfields that could host such radars have published positions. These are, from north to south: INS Kohassa (Shibpur, Diglipur), INS Utkrosh (Port Blair, shared with the civil airport VOPB), Car Nicobar Air Force Station (VOCX) and INS Baaz (Campbell Bay, Great Nicobar; commissioned 31 July 2012). INS Kardip (Kamorta) is a naval base without an airfield, and I found no published coordinates for it. Antenna sites and heights are not published, so the airfield coordinates below are **proxies**, not radar positions. For operating status that night, the only on-record statement is by Rear Admiral Sudhir Pillai, chief of staff of the Andaman and Nicobar Command, to Reuters (about 15–16 March 2014). He said "We have many radar systems operating in this area, but nothing was picked up" and that it was possible the military radars were switched off "as we operate on an 'as required' basis". A separate defence source said India did not keep radars on at all times because of cost [press, Reuters]. A Defence Ministry official, unnamed, later called the idea of a flight through Indian airspace undetected "bizarre" [press, Reuters via Malay Mail, 18 Mar 2014]. Indian sources told the Tribune (17 Mar 2014) that the southern Indian Ocean has "no radar coverage". I found no later formal Indian statement, report or answer in Parliament on Andaman radar status on 7–8 March 2014: **NOT FOUND**. For Port Blair civil ATC, the AAI airport page currently lists "Surveillance: ARSR + MSSR" with the value field shown as "-". I found no source for a Port Blair civil radar or its range in 2014: **NOT FOUND**. Any secondary (MSSR) radar is irrelevant after 17:21, because MH370's transponder was off; only a primary radar could have seen the aircraft. A 2014 blog comment says the Car Nicobar radar was destroyed in the 2004 tsunami and replaced only by an unmanned-at-night mobile radar. This is unverified [independent analyst].

## Q2. The CTBTO IDC infrasound report on MH370

**Short answer.** The report covered the take-off and South China Sea / Malaysia–Vietnam region, not the southern Indian Ocean. Its bulletin search (Fig. 3) used Kuala Lumpur airport (3.125 N, 101.550 E) at 16:41:00 on 7 March 2014 as the source. It searched infrasound arrivals within ±900 s and back-azimuths within ±15° at all stations within 50°. The predicted arrival at I06AU (Cocos, 1,766 km) was 18:15:56. The SEL3 automatic bulletins showed no matching event "within the first 15mn of the flight". Bulletins and interactive review for all stations within 5,000 km of the search area showed no related signal. The network-performance map is for 7 March 18:00, at 1 Hz, for a surface source. The report is undated, but it cites the search area "as of Monday 10 March", so it predates the southern Indian Ocean hypothesis. I found no public IDC search of the southern Indian Ocean: **NOT FOUND**. IS06 (IDC code I06AU; FDSN IM.I06H1–H8) is the correct designation. It was certified and recording in March 2014: the LLNL poster plotted all 8 Cocos sensors for 7–8 March 2014, and the current FDSN epoch starts 31 Oct 2011. The CTBTO report itself calls IS06 relatively noisy, "typical for station on islands". The LLNL CASIS poster "Infrasound and the Search for MH370" is by Ron Kane, Sean Lehman and Jim Candy (CASIS workshop, 21 May 2014) [technical report, not readable]. As described by 370location, it tried to detect the A320 take-offs and landings at Cocos (flights to and from Christmas Island) on 7–8 March and found nothing. 370location attributes the null to time-zone offsets that were added rather than subtracted [independent analyst]. IMS infrasound data for March 2014 are **not public**. EarthScope FDSN returns no data (HTTP 204) for IM.I06H1, I04H1, I07H1 and I52H1 at all dates I tested up to 2 April 2014. Data begin on 3 April 2014 (checked 17 Oct 2026).

## Q3. Would an ocean impact be detectable by infrasound at IS06/IS04/IS07/IS52?

**Short answer.** This is very unlikely on published thresholds, but no study has modelled it for MH370. The kinetic energy for 170–200 t at 100–250 m/s is 0.85–6.25 GJ, or 0.20–1.49 t TNT equivalent (computed). The IMS infrasound design goal is 1 kt [peer-reviewed]. Network simulations give a yearly-average detection threshold of about 500 t, varying seasonally from 50 t to 1 kt. Near the equinoxes (March–May) thresholds rise by about a factor of 4 (Le Pichon et al. 2025, citing Green & Bowers 2010) [peer-reviewed]. For bolides, a 1 kt event has a detection range of about 7,000 km (Silber 2025) [peer-reviewed]. At the stated ranges, the kinetic energy is therefore 33 to 2,500 times below the network threshold before any coupling loss. The fraction of impact energy that goes into the atmosphere is not published for aircraft–ocean impacts: **NOT FOUND**. For the water, one well-recorded airplane impact gives a SOFAR coupling of about 10⁻⁴ (bolide hydroacoustics study, Icarus 2026) [peer-reviewed]. The CTBTO report states that commercial aircraft in normal flight are detected only within about 100 km of a station [official]. The only long-range aircraft case it cites is supersonic Concorde. A close Cocos flyby (tens of km) is the one MH370 scenario in which IS06 data could be decisive. 370location shows a 2019 jet take-off and landing beamformed at I06, with an approach track about 25 km from the array [independent analyst]. I found no published MH370-specific infrasound detectability estimate: **NOT FOUND**. Blackman et al. (2004) did **not** use Cocos receivers. The receivers were IMS hydrophones off Diego Garcia (H08 N/S) and Cape Leeuwin (H01) only [peer-reviewed abstract; UCRL-TR-207323]. No infrasound and no Cocos T-phase records are reported, so noise and received level at Cocos cannot be estimated from that experiment. The 2003 Melville SUS line did end at Cocos Keeling (Blackman et al. 2003, "Cape Town–Cocos Keeling"), but its sources were recorded at the IMS hydrophones.

## Q4. OHASISBIO and the Royer (2016) MH370 report

**Short answer.** OHASISBIO (Observatoire HydroAcoustique de la SISmicité et de la BIOdiversité) is a network of autonomous hydrophones in the southern Indian Ocean. It was run by Jean-Yves Royer (CNRS / Univ. Brest, then Laboratoire Domaines Océaniques; now UMR 6538 Geo-Ocean, IUEM), from Dec 2009/Jan 2010 to its final recovery in 2023. In 2014 it had 6 sites with 8 hydrophones: NCRO, WKER, SWAMS (a 3-hydrophone triangle about 10 km on a side), NEAMS, SSEIR and MAD. All were moored in the SOFAR axis at 970–1,260 m, over 3,310–5,220 m of water. They recorded continuously at 240 Hz (0–120 Hz band) with 24-bit A/D, HTI sensors (−164 dB re 1 µPa/V) and GPS-synchronised clocks (drift about 10⁻⁷). The clock drift could not be determined for MAD and SWAMS-1 [technical report]. Deployment was 11 Jan–16 Feb 2014 (cruise MD197/VT135) and recovery 11 Jan–8 Feb 2015 (MD200/VT141). NEAMS (31°34.12′S 083°11.36′E), the site nearest the presumed impact area, was destroyed during recovery on 1–2 Feb 2015. Royer's report LDO-201608, "Search for the flight MH370 impact site in the Indian Ocean from hydroacoustic data, March 8, 2014", is dated 22 December 2016 and has 18 pages. It combines the OHASISBIO data with H01W and H08S, and tentatively finds 5 acoustic events between 00:16 and 01:06 (Royer's count). Table 2 lists them as 6 location solutions (A, B, C1, C2, D1, D2), because C1/C2 and D1/D2 are mutually exclusive alternative associations of the same records. Two of them match the Los Alamos (Stead) and Curtin events. Royer favours C2 (26.5 S 80.9 E, 00:57) and D2 (27.9 S 93.6 E, 01:06). He concludes that the data "do not unambiguously resolve the enigma of the impact site". Both favoured points lie in seismically active areas, and event A is cryogenic. Royer stresses that the event associations are non-unique, very faint and partly obscured by an airgun survey in the Bay of Bengal. Popular Mechanics (16 July 2026) and Wise's Substack say the analysis was sent to French and Australian investigators in late 2016 but left out of the ATSB 2017 report. Royer is quoted as saying "It didn't bring any new information relative to the CTBTO data". The report is now **public**, through Wise's Google Drive link. I found **no DOI for the 2014 raw waveform data**: a DataCite search for "OHASISBIO" returned only cruise DOIs, mission reports and derived catalogues.
